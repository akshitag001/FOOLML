The model we are attacking

The target is a convolutional neural network (CNN) trained on CIFAR-10, a standard collection of small photos. It looks at a 32×32-pixel colour photo and says which of ten things it shows: airplane, automobile, bird, cat, deer, dog, frog, horse, ship, truck.

03

What is an adversarial example?

An adversarial example is a photo with a tiny, carefully chosen change added to it. The change is too small for a person to notice, but it makes the model give a wrong answer. Below, the middle picture is that change, made ten times stronger so you can see it. Grey means "nothing changed".

04

Types of adversarial attacks

Attacks are usually described by four choices. Each one changes how much the attacker knows or what they are trying to achieve. The last column shows our situation.

QuestionEasier for the attackerHarder for the attackerOurs
What can the attacker see? White-box: the model's internals, including its weights and gradients Black-box: only what goes in and what comes out Black-box
What does the model return? Score-based: a probability for every class ("cat 81%, dog 12%…") Decision-based / hard-label: only the top answer ("cat") Hard-label
What is the goal? Untargeted: any wrong answer will do Targeted: one specific wrong answer, chosen in advance Untargeted
How is the attack found? Transfer-based: attack your own copy of a model and hope it carries over Query-based: ask the real model many times and learn from its answers Query-based

In what we could see, this is the most restricted setting there is: no internals, no scores, just one word per question. Our goal, untargeted, is the easier of the two goals. Any wrong answer counts.

05

The rules of our challenge

Allowed

  • Send a photo to the model and read its answer
  • Do this as many times as needed (fewer is better)
  • Use the clean photos we were given

Not allowed

  • Looking at the model's weights or architecture
  • Looking at its internal layers or gradients
  • Looking at its probabilities or scores

How we are scored

50%Attack success rate: how many saved images the model gets wrong
50%Imperceptibility: how invisible the changes are
tie-breakFewer queries to the model, and a larger change in the model's confidence

Every adversarial image must be saved as a normal PNG file, one per original photo, named like cat_5_adv.png.

06

How our attack works

Think of every possible photo as a point on a huge map. The model has divided the map into ten regions, one per answer. The line between two regions is the decision boundary. Our clean cat photo sits inside the "cat" region. We want the closest point just across the line: close means a small change, and across the line means a wrong answer. The catch is that we cannot see the map. We can only ask, for any point, "which region is this?"

  1. Find any photo the model gets wrong

    We try random static until the model calls it something other than the true class. If that fails, we use the nearest photo of another class. This is our starting point. It is wrong, but very far from the original.

  2. Binary search to the boundary

    We blend the clean photo and the starting point. Halfway still wrong? Move closer to the clean photo. Correct again? Move back. About a dozen questions land us right on the boundary.

  3. Walk along the boundary toward the clean photo

    This is HopSkipJumpAttack. Around the current point we ask about a hundred slightly jiggled versions. From which ones come back "wrong" and which come back "right", we estimate which way the boundary runs. Then we step along it and search back onto the line. Each step makes the change smaller.

  4. Polish

    We tested three refinements: smoother low-frequency noise, hiding the noise in busy textured areas, and a small safety margin past the boundary. We kept only those that helped. Section 07 has the numbers.

  5. Save and check again

    PNG files store whole numbers from 0 to 255, so saving rounds every pixel. That rounding can undo a very small change. We reload every saved file, ask the model again, and push any image that slipped back a little further.

Technical detail: the maths, simply

The change. Each photo is a list of 3072 numbers between 0 and 1. The adversarial photo is xadv = x + δ, where δ is the change we add. We want δ as small as possible while the model's answer differs from the true label.

Binary search. For a blend weight α between 0 and 1, we test x(α) = (1 − α)·x + α·xstart. If x(α) is still wrong we lower α, otherwise we raise it. Each question halves the interval.

Boundary direction from labels only (HSJA). At a point b on the boundary we draw n random directions u1…un, ask about b + δ·ui, and score each answer +1 (wrong) or −1 (right). The average of scorei·ui, after subtracting the mean score, points away from the correct region, roughly perpendicular to the boundary. We step along it, then binary-search back to the boundary. The number of directions grows each round (100·√t).

Measuring the change.

  • L2: overall size of the change, the square root of the sum of squared pixel differences (pixels from 0 to 1). Lower is better.
  • L∞: the biggest change to any single pixel value. Lower is better.
  • SSIM: structural similarity, 1.0 means identical to the eye. Higher is better.
  • PSNR: signal-to-noise ratio in decibels. Above about 40 dB the change is generally invisible.

09

Challenges and limitations

  • Rounding can undo an attack. Saved images store whole numbers from 0 to 255. Some of our changes were smaller than a single step, so saving erased them. We had to make every change survive rounding, and check it again after saving.
  • The same model can answer differently. On graphics cards, a faster lower-precision mode (TF32) can flip the answer for an image right on the boundary, depending on how many images are checked together. We used full precision and a small safety margin, and confirmed every image on both the graphics card and the CPU.
  • Noise against questions. More questions buy a smaller change, but each one costs time and counts against us in a tie-break. Going from 1000 to 5000 questions per photo shrank our median change to about a third.
  • We don't know the exact scoring metric. "Imperceptibility" could mean L2, L∞, SSIM or something else. Hiding noise in textured areas raised SSIM slightly but made L2 larger, so the best choice depends on the metric.
  • Some photos are much harder than others. A few need almost no change; others, often plain backgrounds or high-contrast objects, need many times more.

10

Why this matters

Image classifiers increasingly make real decisions: moderating uploads, reading road signs, checking identity documents. Often all an outsider can do is submit an input and see the decision. That is exactly the setting here. Showing that a handful of questions and an invisible change are enough to flip the answer is a reminder that accuracy on clean photos says little about security. The same method works as a test: anyone building a defence can run label-only attacks like this one against it and measure how much change it takes to break.
